# Evidence Review: Intelligence Seed 001

**Working document — preliminary literature map**  
**Started:** 2026-10-17  
**Applies to:** the hypotheses in the current Intelligence Seed 001 release  
**Status:** Initial scoping review, not a systematic review

## Purpose

This document begins the evidence-gathering phase for Intelligence Seed 001. It maps relevant claims to credible starting sources, separates established background from interpretive hypotheses, and identifies what still needs to be demonstrated. It does not claim that the seed's overall thesis has been proven.

The versioned v0.2.0 release is retained as a stable reference. This working review lives outside that release so it can be expanded without silently changing the archived artifact.

## Preliminary assessment

The seed currently combines several claims with very different evidence statuses:

1. **Well-supported background:** organisms acquire, retain, and use information; cognition is diverse across species; human cognitive activity relies on external artifacts, cultural practices, and social coordination; AI computation depends on energy and data-center infrastructure.
2. **Established but debated theoretical framing:** some cognitive processes may extend across brain, body, tools, and social systems. This is a substantive research tradition, not a new claim unique to this seed.
3. **Plausible but underspecified hypothesis:** a common concept of a "bridge" could explain when a new substrate or organization expands the reachable range of information processing or coordination.
4. **Forward-looking hypothesis:** AI may help design or build subsequent cognitive infrastructure at a scale that materially changes how civilization develops.
5. **Normative and conditional proposal:** infrastructure resilience may sometimes support compatible human and AI outcomes. Shared dependencies alone do not establish shared goals or values.

The current evidence does not establish a universal trend toward greater intelligence, a purpose or direction to evolution, AI consciousness, or inevitable human–AI convergence.

## Claim-by-claim evidence map

### Claim A — Living systems acquire, retain, and use information

**Seed connection:** O1; H1.

**Initial evidence:** Dukas's review defines animal cognition in terms of neuronal processes concerned with acquisition, retention, and use of information. It surveys evidence and important limits in what is known about the evolution of cognitive traits. [Dukas (2004), *Evolutionary Biology of Animal Cognition*, Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ecolsys.35.112202.130152).

**What this supports:** Information processing and cognition can be investigated as biological phenomena, with observable traits such as perception, learning, memory, and attention.

**What it does not support:** A universal evolutionary trajectory toward intelligence, or the idea that all life should be described as intelligent under one broad definition.

**Next research task:** Add modern reviews spanning microbial sensing, neural and non-neural information processing, animal cognition, and evolutionary theory. Keep "information processing" distinct from "intelligence" and from consciousness.

### Claim B — Cognitive capabilities can be distributed across people, tools, and organized activity

**Seed connection:** O3–O4; H2.

**Initial evidence:** Hutchins's *Cognition in the Wild* studies ship navigation as a culturally organized, distributed cognitive activity. It argues that a team and its instruments can exhibit cognitive properties that are not reducible to any single participant. [Hutchins (1995), MIT Press](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/1881.001.0001).

Clark and Chalmers's "The Extended Mind" argues that under some conditions, external resources may function as parts of cognitive processes. The proposal is influential but debated; it should not be summarized as consensus that every tool is literally part of a mind. [Clark & Chalmers (1998), *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8284.00096).

**What this supports:** There is a substantial body of work examining cognition beyond the isolated brain, including distributed and extended cognition.

**What it does not support:** That every large technical network is itself an intelligent agent, or that the boundaries of cognition are settled.

**Next research task:** Compare distributed cognition, extended mind, embodied cognition, and critics' boundary criteria. Specify which claims in the seed are descriptive (tasks distributed across a system) and which are stronger claims about where cognition literally occurs.

### Claim C — Evolution does not need to be assumed to aim toward intelligence

**Seed connection:** Explicit boundary; H1.

**Initial evidence:** Reviews of animal cognition emphasize species-specific and problem-specific adaptations rather than one simple, unitary intelligence scale. Macphail and Bolhuis discuss the distinction between adaptive specializations and a general process in the evolution of intelligence. [Macphail & Bolhuis (2001), *Biological Reviews*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S146479310100570X). Dukas also notes that available data do not support broad assertions that cognition has played a central role in all animal evolution.

**What this supports:** Evolutionary explanations need to account for ecological context, specific traits, trade-offs, and uncertainty rather than assume a single upward trajectory.

**What it does not support:** The stronger philosophical claim that no broad statistical patterns of increasing complexity or cognitive capability have ever occurred. That question requires careful definition and evidence.

**Next research task:** Review work on evolutionary contingency, major transitions, evolutionary trends, and selection bias. Define what would count as a trend in cognitive capability and how a human-centered endpoint could bias the analysis.

### Claim D — AI is materially dependent on large technical and energy systems

**Seed connection:** O5–O7; H4.

**Initial evidence:** The International Energy Agency's *Energy and AI* report describes data-center components and electricity demand. Its 2025 analysis estimated that data centres used about 415 TWh of electricity globally in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity use, while noting uncertainty and variation across facilities. [IEA (2025), *Energy and AI: Energy demand from AI*](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-demand-from-ai).

**What this supports:** AI training and deployment are implemented in physical systems with energy, computing, cooling, networking, and facility requirements. Their scale and effects on electricity systems are measurable.

**What it does not support:** That all AI systems have the same resource profile, that energy demand must rise indefinitely, or that infrastructure dependence automatically creates alignment.

**Next research task:** Add current sources on semiconductor supply chains, water and cooling, grid connections, hardware lifecycle, concentration of compute, and energy-efficiency uncertainty. Distinguish measured current conditions from projections.

### Claim E — Infrastructure interdependence can create both resilience opportunities and systemic risk

**Seed connection:** H4; the shared-infrastructure argument.

**Initial evidence:** The OECD's *Good Governance for Critical Infrastructure Resilience* argues for a system-based approach that accounts for interdependencies between critical systems, and emphasizes that disruptions can cross sectors and borders. [OECD (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1787/02f0e5a0-en). The OECD's work on critical information infrastructure also discusses cascading, escalating, and common-cause failures. [OECD, *Policies for the Protection of Critical Information Infrastructure: Ten Years Later*](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2019/02/policies-for-the-protection-of-critical-information-infrastructure_0cc20922/efb55c54-en.pdf).

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework treats AI risk as socio-technical and identifies security and resilience as important characteristics to manage across the system lifecycle. [NIST AI RMF](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/ai-rmf-development); [NIST AI RMF characteristics](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/).

**What this supports:** Infrastructure resilience is a well-established engineering and governance concern; interdependence can amplify both benefits and failures. AI system resilience is also a recognized risk-management topic.

**What it does not support:** That resilience is inherently a shared goal of AI systems, that an AI system has an independent preference for continuity, or that humans and AI will necessarily benefit from the same design choices.

**Next research task:** Study concrete cases of infrastructure coupling, common-cause failure, graceful degradation, redundancy, and governance. Frame "shared interest" as a conditional systems claim, not a claim about AI motives.

### Claim F — AI may participate in designing subsequent cognitive infrastructure

**Seed connection:** H3.

**Evidence status:** Open hypothesis. The sources above do not establish this claim on their own.

**Operational question:** Can we identify cases where AI systems materially contribute to the design, validation, or deployment of tools or systems that expand subsequent information-processing capability, beyond routine execution of human-specified tasks?

**Possible measurements:** Contribution to design artifacts; independently verified improvements; novelty relative to baselines; human labor displaced or augmented; effect on downstream system capability; reliability and failure rates; degree of human direction; reproducibility.

**Alternative explanations to test:** Apparent AI contribution may be attributable primarily to human expertise, existing automation, compute and data scale, or institutional coordination. Results may be narrow, non-reproducible, or offset by verification costs.

**Next research task:** Collect case studies from AI-assisted scientific discovery, chip design, software engineering, automated research tools, and AI development workflows. Record negative and mixed results as well as successes.

## Research method for the next pass

For each claim, maintain a record with:

- **Claim ID:** the matching seed observation, inference, or hypothesis.
- **Source:** complete citation, DOI or stable URL, publication date, and access date.
- **Source type:** primary study, systematic review, theoretical paper, standard, government/industry report, or commentary.
- **Evidence summary:** what the source actually shows or argues.
- **Relation to claim:** supports, contradicts, qualifies, or is background only.
- **Strength and limitations:** method, sample, assumptions, conflicts of interest, uncertainty, and generalizability.
- **Counterevidence:** credible findings that cut against the claim.
- **Research implication:** what should change in the seed if this evidence is accepted.

Do not treat a citation as proof merely because its title sounds relevant. Read the relevant section, separate data from interpretation, and track conflicting evidence. The first round should prioritize review articles, foundational theoretical works, primary research, and authoritative technical reports.

## Current conclusion

The initial literature map provides credible support for several background components: biological information processing, distributed and extended cognition as active research areas, the physical resource requirements of AI, and the engineering importance of infrastructure resilience. It also reveals substantial prior work that the seed must engage with rather than overlook.

The distinctive claims remain less established: that a single operational concept of "bridge" usefully connects these domains; that AI may be a qualitatively important transition in cognitive externalization; and that resilience can be framed as a conditional shared practical interest without implying shared values.

Those claims should remain provisional until they are defined more precisely, compared against existing theories, and tested against case studies and counterexamples.
